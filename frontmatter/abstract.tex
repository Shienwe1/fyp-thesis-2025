\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}
 
This project addresses a critical implementation gap in computational electromagnetics by developing an open-source finite element solver for light scattering simulation using high-order N\'{e}d\'{e}lec edge elements within the FEniCSx framework. The first objective focused on solver development and stabilisation, to implement a high-accuracy finite element solver utilising unstructured meshes and vector edge elements to ensure physical correctness and geometric flexibility. The methodology employs third-order $H(\text{curl})$-conforming N\'{e}d\'{e}lec elements on unstructured triangular meshes to discretise the time-harmonic vector Helmholtz equation. The second objective was to verify the solver accuracy against the analytical Mie series benchmark for a two-dimensional perfect electric conductor cylinder. Quantitative validation revealed that the solver operates within the stable asymptotic regime where $kh = 0.374$, avoiding numerical pollution effects. The mesh convergence study provided definitive proof of implementation correctness, demonstrating that the numerical error decreases monotonically with mesh refinement and achieves an empirical convergence rate of 2.88, in excellent agreement with the theoretical prediction of 3.0 for cubic N\'{e}d\'{e}lec elements. The third objective was to validate robust domain truncation techniques, specifically absorbing boundary conditions to simulate an unbounded free-space environment and extend these concepts into localized surface plasmon resonance biosensing applications. Upgrading the domain truncation boundary to an Anisotropic Perfectly Matched Layer successfully eliminated boundary-induced reflection noise, reducing the final global relative error to 0.019\%. When deployed to model a penetrable 25 nm radius gold core-shell nanoparticle, the volume-source formulation accurately tracked the macroscopic optical transduction, yielding a highly linear bulk sensitivity of 321.64 nm/RIU with a coefficient of determination of 0.99523. Furthermore, the parametric surface decay analysis extracted a characteristic evanescent confinement length of exactly 5.1 nm, providing rigorous data-driven validation of the near-field plasmonic physics. This research directly advances United Nations Sustainable Development Goals by democratising professional-grade computational tools for researchers and students without advanced programming skills, and lowering financial barriers to support global scientific innovation and equitable engineering education.
