\chapter{Introduction}

\section{Introduction to Light Scattering Simulation}

Light scattering is a ubiquitous and fundamental physical phenomenon that describes the interaction of electromagnetic waves with discrete particles or inhomogeneous media. Whether it is the blue color of the sky, the brilliance of a white cloud, or the operation of radar systems, scattering occurs whenever an incident electromagnetic wave encounters an obstacle. This interaction induces oscillating multipoles—microscopic electric charge separations that vary with time—within the object, which subsequently radiate secondary waves known as the scattered field. Understanding how the amplitude, phase, and polarization of light evolve during this interaction is essential for a wide array of scientific and engineering disciplines.

The detailed study of these interactions is a cornerstone for the development of advanced technologies. In remote sensing, the analysis of scattered waves allows for the detection and characterization of objects that are otherwise inaccessible. Similarly, in biomedical optics, the scattering properties of tissues are exploited to diagnose diseases at an early stage. However, the complexity of real-world scatterers, which often possess irregular shapes and inhomogeneous material properties, renders analytical solutions such as the Mie series insufficient. While Mie theory provides an exact mathematical solution for scattering by simple spheres and has served as the gold standard for validation in the field \cite{Wriedt2012}, it cannot be applied to the arbitrary geometries encountered in practical engineering problems. Consequently, numerical simulation has become an indispensable tool. By discretizing the governing equations of electromagnetism—converting the continuous differential equations into a system of algebraic equations solvable by computer—numerical methods allow researchers to predict scattering behavior with high fidelity \cite{Mittra1991}.

The physics of this interaction is governed entirely by the laws of classical electromagnetism. At the macroscopic level, the behavior of these electromagnetic fields is described by a set of coupled partial differential equations known as Maxwell's equations. These equations provide the rigorous framework linking the electric field ($\mathbf{E}$) and magnetic field ($\mathbf{H}$) to the material properties of the scatterer, serving as the foundation for all light scattering simulations.

\subsection{Governing Principles: Maxwell's Equations}

In the context of light scattering, the fundamental behavior of electromagnetic fields is described by Maxwell's equations. For a linear, isotropic medium characterized by permittivity $\epsilon$ (a measure of electric polarizability), permeability $\mu$ (a measure of magnetic response), and conductivity $\sigma$, the general time-domain differential forms of these equations are:

\begin{align}
\nabla \times \mathbf{E} &= -\frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t} \label{eq:faraday} \\
\nabla \times \mathbf{H} &= \frac{\partial \mathbf{D}}{\partial t} + \mathbf{J} \label{eq:ampere} \\
\nabla \cdot \mathbf{D} &= \rho \label{eq:gauss_e} \\
\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} &= 0 \label{eq:gauss_m}
\end{align}

where $\mathbf{D} = \epsilon \mathbf{E}$ is the electric flux density and $\mathbf{B} = \mu \mathbf{H}$ is the magnetic flux density. Each equation encapsulates a specific aspect of the physical behavior. Faraday's Law of Induction (\ref{eq:faraday}) describes electromagnetic induction, stating that a spatially varying rotational electric field is coupled to the time rate of change of the magnetic field. In scattering, this mechanism allows the incident wave to induce time-varying fields that propagate through the medium. The Maxwell-Ampère Law (\ref{eq:ampere}) describes how a magnetic field circulation is generated by both conduction current density ($\mathbf{J}$) and displacement current density ($\partial \mathbf{D}/\partial t$). Together with Faraday's law, it creates the self-sustaining mechanism that allows electromagnetic energy to propagate as a wave. The interplay between $\epsilon$ and $\mu$ dictates the wave's phase velocity ($v = 1/\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}$) and intrinsic impedance ($\eta = \sqrt{\mu/\epsilon}$), which determine reflection and transmission characteristics at interfaces. Finally, Gauss's Laws (\ref{eq:gauss_e} \& \ref{eq:gauss_m}) enforce the conservation of flux. Equation (\ref{eq:gauss_m}) dictates that magnetic field lines form continuous loops, reflecting the absence of magnetic monopoles, while Equation (\ref{eq:gauss_e}) relates electric flux divergence to charge density. In source-free regions ($\rho=0$), both fields must be divergence-free ($\nabla \cdot \mathbf{E} = 0$), implying that the fields form continuous lines of flux without sources or sinks. Enforcing this constraint is a critical challenge in numerical simulations, as failure to do so results in spurious modes—non-physical mathematical solutions that do not satisfy the divergence-free condition.

Maxwell's equations alone are insufficient to define a unique solution; they must be supplemented by boundary conditions that describe the physics at the limits of the computational domain and at material interfaces. In the development of a Finite Element solver, three types of conditions are paramount. First, interface conditions ensure continuity at the boundary between two different media (e.g., air and a dielectric scatterer), where the tangential components (parallel to the surface) of the electric and magnetic fields must be continuous ($\hat{n} \times (\mathbf{E}_1 - \mathbf{E}_2) = 0$). Critically, the normal components (perpendicular to the surface) are generally discontinuous due to surface charges or polarization differences. Standard "nodal" finite elements, which enforce continuity of all vector components, fail to model this discontinuity correctly, necessitating the use of Vector Edge Elements (Nédélec elements) \cite{Nedelec1980}. Second, to model ideal metallic scatterers or mirrors, the Perfect Electric Conductor (PEC) condition is applied, forcing the tangential electric field to zero on the surface ($\hat{n} \times \mathbf{E} = 0$). Third, since light scattering typically occurs in an unbounded open space, a radiation condition is required. Computer simulations utilize a finite domain $\Omega$, so the outer boundary of the mesh must be transparent to outgoing waves to mimic an infinite environment. This is mathematically formulated as the Sommerfeld radiation condition and approximated in Finite Element Analysis using Absorbing Boundary Conditions (ABCs) or Perfectly Matched Layers (PMLs) \cite{Berenger1994} applied at the truncation boundary to suppress artificial reflections.

\subsection{Numerical Solving Methods}

While analytical solutions like the Mie series exist for simple geometries, real-world engineering problems often involve complex shapes and inhomogeneous materials that require numerical approximation. Broadly, numerical solvers are categorized by the domain in which they operate—Time Domain or Frequency Domain—and the discretization technique they employ. Time-Domain Solvers, such as the Finite Difference Time Domain (FDTD), simulate the evolution of electromagnetic fields at discrete time steps. They are particularly effective for transient analysis and wideband characterization, as a single simulation run can provide information over a broad frequency spectrum via Fourier transform. However, they can suffer from numerical dispersion errors and require small time steps for stability (CFL condition).

Conversely, Frequency-Domain Solvers assume a time-harmonic dependence ($e^{j\omega t}$) and solve for the steady-state field distribution at a single frequency. Techniques like the Method of Moments (MoM) or Boundary Element Method (BEM) discretize only the surface of the scatterer, offering high accuracy for homogeneous scatterers but becoming computationally expensive for complex materials due to dense matrices \cite{Buffa2003}. The Finite Element Method (FEM) discretizes the entire volume, excelling at modeling complex geometries and inhomogeneous materials due to its unstructured mesh capability. Recent work has demonstrated that FEM can provide highly accurate predictions for challenging scenarios, such as electromagnetic scattering by nanoparticles, where capturing strong field gradients is critical \cite{Grand2020-we}. However, it generates large linear systems that can be computationally intensive to solve. Finite Difference Frequency Domain (FDFD) is similar to FDTD but applied to steady-state equations; while straightforward to implement, it often struggles with curved boundaries due to the geometric approximation error inherent in rectangular grids \cite{Rumpf2014}. Choosing the appropriate solver depends on the specific requirements of the problem, such as the need for broadband data, the complexity of the geometry, and the electrical size of the scatterer.

\subsection{Significance of Light Scattering Simulation}

The ability to accurately simulate light scattering interactions drives continuous technological advancement and fundamental research across diverse fields. In biomedical imaging, non-invasive diagnostic techniques such as Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT) rely heavily on the analysis of backscattering patterns. By accurately modeling how light interacts with cellular structures, researchers can detect subtle morphological changes associated with early-stage cancer, enabling differentiation between healthy and malignant tissues without the need for invasive biopsies. This capability is directly dependent on the fidelity of the underlying scattering models, which must account for the complex, inhomogeneous nature of biological tissue.

In the realm of atmospheric physics and climate science, light scattering plays a pivotal role in the Earth's energy balance. Global climate models depend on accurate simulations of sunlight scattering off ice crystals and aerosols in cirrus clouds to calculate the Earth's radiation budget. The irregular shapes of these particles defy simple analytical solutions, making rigorous numerical simulation essential for reducing uncertainties in climate predictions. Furthermore, in the rapidly evolving field of photonics, the design of next-generation devices such as metamaterials, stealth technologies, and optical sensors hinges on the precise manipulation of scattering cross-sections. Simulation tools allow engineers to "inverse design" structures with specific optical properties, pushing the scientific boundary by enabling the exploration of complex geometries and material properties that are difficult or impossible to fabricate and test experimentally without prior validation \cite{Rumpf2014}.

\subsection{Current Challenges in Simulation}

Despite the variety of available numerical methods, significant computational hurdles remain in the accurate simulation of light scattering, particularly for frequency-domain approaches. One of the primary challenges is the so-called "pollution effect" or dispersion error that accumulates in large-scale problems involving geometries much larger than the optical wavelength \cite{Melenk2010}. This requires a very fine mesh density to maintain phase accuracy, leading to massive systems of equations.

A specific challenge for frequency-domain solvers is the nature of the resulting linear system. Unlike the positive-definite systems found in static structural mechanics, the discretization of the frequency-domain Helmholtz equation leads to an indefinite complex symmetric linear system ($K - k^2M$). These indefinite systems are notoriously difficult to solve because standard iterative solvers like Conjugate Gradient do not converge, and more robust solvers like GMRES can stagnate or require expensive preconditioning \cite{Aruliah2002}. Furthermore, while frequency-domain methods are ideal for single-frequency or resonant analysis, they can be inefficient for broadband characterization compared to time-domain methods, as the entire system matrix must be solved repeatedly for every frequency point of interest.

However, these challenges can be effectively mitigated within the Finite Element framework. The use of high-order vector edge elements significantly reduces dispersion error (pollution) compared to low-order elements, allowing for coarser meshes. Additionally, the geometric flexibility of FEM allows for adaptive meshing, placing elements only where needed to capture rapid field variations, which optimizes the problem size. Finally, modern sparse direct solvers (like multifrontal methods) have become powerful enough to handle the indefinite systems arising from 2D scattering problems efficiently, making Frequency-Domain FEM a robust choice for high-fidelity, steady-state analysis.

\section{Problem Statement}

While various commercial software packages exist for electromagnetic simulation, their "black-box" nature fundamentally limits scientific verification and optimization. Users are often restricted to pre-defined algorithms and meshing strategies without access to the underlying mathematical formulation or source code. This opacity prevents researchers from rigorously verifying accuracy against analytical benchmarks or implementing custom features such as specialized boundary conditions or high-order elements tailored for specific research needs. Furthermore, general-purpose commercial solvers often prioritize versatility over specific accuracy in light scattering problems, potentially masking critical errors such as spurious modes or artificial reflections that can corrupt high-fidelity optical simulations.

This lack of transparency and control gives rise to a critical need for a custom, verifiable simulation framework. Specifically, the development of an open-source Frequency-Domain Finite Element Method (FD-FEM) solver is required to address the following interconnected challenges that degrade the accuracy of conventional methods:

The primary challenge lies in \textbf{geometric fidelity and mesh adaptability}. Structured grid methods like Finite Difference Frequency Domain (FDFD) rely on Cartesian grids, which leads to geometric approximation errors when modeling curved or irregular boundaries as noted by Rumpf et al. \cite{Rumpf2014}. This degradation in accuracy is particularly problematic for high-fidelity applications unless extremely fine grids are used, significantly increasing computational cost. A custom FEM solver allows for the direct implementation of unstructured triangular meshes that can conform perfectly to complex geometries, eliminating staircasing errors.

Furthermore, light scattering occurs in an infinite open space, but computer simulations are inherently finite. Simply cutting off the domain causes artificial reflections from the boundaries that contaminate the solution. A robust Absorbing Boundary Condition (ABC), such as those derived by Engquist and Majda \cite{Engquist1977}, or a Perfectly Matched Layer (PML) pioneered by Berenger \cite{Berenger1994}, is required to mimic an infinite environment without introducing non-physical reflections. Implementing these directly ensures control over the truncation error.

Another critical issue involves \textbf{vector field stability and spurious modes}. Electromagnetic waves are vector fields where the normal component of the electric field is discontinuous at material interfaces. Standard nodal finite element methods force continuity in all directions, violating this physics and producing non-physical spurious modes that corrupt the solution \cite{Paulsen1994}. The use of edge elements has been established as a critical solution to this issue \cite{Nedelec1980, Zhong2018}. A custom solver ensures the correct mathematical basis functions are used to preserve physical laws. Finally, the frequency-domain Helmholtz equation leads to an indefinite linear system ($K - k^2M$) that is computationally expensive to solve. Unlike positive-definite systems found in static problems, these indefinite systems cannot be solved efficiently with standard Conjugate Gradient methods, necessitating the use of robust sparse direct solvers or specialized preconditioners \cite{Aruliah2002}.

In summary, reliance on opaque commercial software hinders the ability to guarantee physical correctness in complex scattering problems. The core problem this project addresses is the development of a transparent, high-fidelity solver that rigorously overcomes geometric approximation errors, eliminates spurious solutions through correct vector basis formulation, and effectively truncates unbounded domains, providing a verifiable benchmark for optical engineering applications.

\section{Research Objectives}

The overarching goal of this research is to develop and validate a robust Frequency-Domain Finite Element (FD-FEM) solver for 2D light scattering simulations. The specific objectives are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mathematical Formulation and Approximation:} To derive the frequency-domain variational formulation of the vector Helmholtz equation by applying the weighted residual method and Green's theorems. This objective involves converting the strong form of the governing partial differential equations into a weak form suitable for finite element approximation, establishing the rigorous mathematical foundation required to model steady-state wave propagation in complex, inhomogeneous media \cite{Jin2015}.
    
    \item \textbf{Solver Development and Stabilization:} To develop a high-accuracy Frequency-Domain Finite Element solver utilizing unstructured meshes and Vector Edge Elements (Nédélec elements) \cite{Nedelec1980} to ensure physical correctness and geometric flexibility. This development focuses on correctly enforcing the physical continuity of tangential electric fields while permitting normal discontinuities at material interfaces, thereby eliminating the non-physical spurious modes that plague traditional nodal-based solvers and ensuring vector field stability.
    
    \item \textbf{Domain Truncation and Validation:} To implement robust domain truncation techniques, specifically Absorbing Boundary Conditions (ABC), to simulate an unbounded free-space environment within a finite computational domain. This includes validating the solver's accuracy by benchmarking the computed Radar Cross Section (RCS) against exact analytical Mie series solutions for canonical geometries, utilizing validation standards for near-field and far-field accuracy as discussed by \cite{Grand2020-we}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Scope of Work}

This project focuses on the development of a Two-Dimensional (2D) electromagnetic solver for macroscopic Maxwell's equations in linear, isotropic, and non-magnetic media. The analysis is strictly confined to the \textbf{Frequency Domain}, assuming a time-harmonic dependence ($e^{j\omega t}$) for all fields. This approach is chosen to directly compute the steady-state scattering response without the need for time-stepping algorithms.

The geometric scope includes the simulation of scattering from dielectric and metallic cylinders with arbitrary cross-sections. The solver will utilize the \textbf{Scattered Field (SF)} formulation, which separates the total field into a known incident wave and an unknown scattered wave, facilitating the direct application of radiation conditions. The numerical methodology relies on \textbf{First-Order Vector Edge Elements} on unstructured triangular meshes to handle complex geometries and enforce correct vector field continuity.

Computationally, the project involves assembling global stiffness and mass matrices to form a linear system of equations ($Ax=b$). This system, characteristic of the frequency-domain Helmholtz equation, is indefinite and complex-symmetric. It will be solved using sparse direct solvers (such as LU decomposition) to ensure robustness and accuracy. Validation of the solver is limited to the optical and microwave frequency regimes, specifically benchmarking against the analytical Mie series solution for a dielectric cylinder. Nonlinear optical effects, time-varying material properties, and transient time-domain phenomena are explicitly excluded from this study.
